How to prepare a script you can deliver to the camera

On camera, people are watching your face. Write lines you could say to one person, keep the next sentence close to the lens, and check the picture and sound before you start.

By LiWen Chen

Published Nov 26, 2026, 10:36

When you record a short update, leave a video message, or speak with the camera on, people are watching your face. If the script is on the desk, or well below the lens, every glance down looks like reading.

The problem is not “never use a script.” It is writing lines you could say to one person, placing the next line close to the lens, and checking the picture and sound before you start.

Write it as something you would say to one person

A camera script is not a memo to be read aloud. Viewers cannot glance back at a long sentence they just missed, so each line should carry one idea, in words you would actually say to a colleague.

Decide what they should remember or do, then write the opening. A formal introduction can keep your name and role when the occasion needs it. A short work update often starts with the decision, not a long greeting.

The following is a fictional teaching example. It does not describe a real team, project, or announcement. It only contrasts a written notice with a script you can say to camera.

Before:

Hi everyone, I am a member of the project team, and I wanted to take a few minutes today to walk you through our recent work. We have had several rounds of discussion and coordination, overall progress has been fairly smooth, and there are also a few items that still need further confirmation. I will briefly go over them below, and I hope you will take a look when you have a moment.

After:

This week I only need to confirm one thing: I suggest we keep the pilot to two workflows, and not add more scope.

The instruction page already reflects the first round of feedback. What we still need is the sample date. If we cannot confirm it by Wednesday, next week’s test will have to move.

Please reply before Wednesday if you can provide the sample. Once I hear back, I will send the updated schedule to the group.

The revised version splits the judgment, the condition, and the request into sentences you can finish in one breath. “Two workflows” and “Wednesday” belong only to this example. When you edit your own script, replace them with a scope and a date you can actually stand behind.

Then check:

  • Write numbers, names, product names, and abbreviations the way you will say them. The first time you use an abbreviation, give the wording your audience already knows.
  • Keep must-be-accurate lines in full sentences. Leave optional examples as a cue, such as “add one situation I have seen.”
  • Do not pack background, action, and a request into a single sentence. If a listener misses the second half, the whole point is gone.

If this is a five-minute meeting update, use How to prepare and rehearse a five-minute work update for structure and timing. If you will also show slides, first separate what the audience should read from what you will say, as in How to turn your slides into a script you can actually say, then come back to the camera setup.

Put the next line close to the lens

The University of Washington Office of Admissions’ webcam tips say to place the camera at about eye level, keep your eyes in the upper third of the frame, and look at the camera, not the preview of yourself. The open Public Speaking course from Lumen Learning makes the same point: looking at the webcam is how you look at the audience; looking at faces on the screen, or at your own picture, is not.

Toastmasters International’s 2023–2024 guidance for online and hybrid speech contests states that, for a contestant presenting online, looking at the camera is considered eye contact, and it recommends placing the camera at eye level if possible. That is contest preparation guidance, not a test of every meeting app, and you do not need to be in a contest to use the same setup.

In the April 2021 Toastmaster magazine, Lindy MacLaine notes that a camera often gives you no live reaction. Her suggestion is to treat the lens as a person you are trying to reach—even a small photo of someone you trust, placed at the “right” eye-contact spot beside the camera, can change how you speak. That is her preparation method, not an experiment we have run.

A practical setup looks like this:

  1. Raise the camera to eye level, so the viewer is not looking up at your chin or down at your forehead.
  2. Put the script as close to the lens as you can. A dedicated teleprompter mirror is one option. Without one, a phone or tablet just below or just above the lens shortens the glance away from the camera.
  3. Keep each line short, so your eyes do not travel far across the screen. This is a practical way to reduce obvious scanning, not a measured line-width standard.
  4. Before you record or join the meeting, check that you are looking at the lens, not at the script, a participant tile, or your self-view.

If you also need to show a deck, share only the slides or the browser window, and check the audience preview. Sharing the entire desktop can include your prompt.

Landscape or portrait depends on where the video will be watched. Frame the shot in the orientation you will actually use. There is no single “more professional” direction for every video.

Check the picture and the sound before you start

The University of Washington tips also recommend testing your setup on the platform you will use, finding a quiet place, lighting your face from the front rather than from behind, and being careful with noises that a laptop microphone can exaggerate when you touch the computer. The same Public Speaking course warns that a window behind you can turn your face into a silhouette, and that in many online talks, sound matters more than a perfect shot. These are preparation reminders, not tests we performed.

You can start with the site’s online webcam test to check framing, background, and lighting. The page requests camera access only after you start. It does not request the microphone, and it does not record or save the picture. A mirrored preview changes only what you see on that page, not how another app will display it. Then use the online microphone test: say a sentence at your normal volume and watch whether the input meter moves with your voice. The meter is not a quality score, and it does not prove that someone else will hear you clearly.

Both tools only check input in the current browser. After you stop the test, select the same devices again in the meeting or recording app and do a short check there. Other apps may crop or flip the picture, or use different permissions. A browser preview does not replace that step.

Rehearse out loud, then record or go live to the lens

Once the script is ready, paste it into the speech length estimator to see whether the update is likely to run long. The estimate is not your actual speaking time. Planned pauses, a prop, or a cut to another shot need their own allowance.

If a sentence still sounds like a document, or you are unsure of a name or a number, preview the wording in Hear your script before you speak. How long the synthetic voice takes is not how long you will take. The tools do not carry a script from one page to another, so keep your own copy before you leave. Do not paste confidential material into a website tool.

Then rehearse in your own voice, in three passes:

  1. First pass: finish and time it. Speak at a normal volume from the opening to the close. If you stumble, keep going, and write down the total time.
  2. Second pass: look at the lens. Sit or stand at the distance and orientation you will actually use. Note which line makes you drop or dart your eyes. Shorten that line, or move the script closer to the lens, instead of speeding up.
  3. Third pass: use the real setup. Check the picture and sound, then deliver the whole piece once. A recorded message can be captured in sections; you do not have to chase a single perfect take. A live meeting or livestream still needs a complete run-through, including how you pick up the next line after a pause. For returning after a detour, see How to find your next line after you go off script.

In the July 2025 Toastmaster magazine, Faith Bradley notes that a remote audience can slip into another window, so the hook, a transition, and the “so what” are worth saying with clearer emphasis. That is delivery advice, not a measured completion-rate result. It is not a reason to speak a fixed percentage faster, or to rush the script to sound more energetic.

If you need a prompt, keep it close to the lens, and do not share it with the audience

What belongs in the teleprompter is the spoken script. The audience, or the finished recording, should not see that window unless you are deliberately showing the setup.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a word-for-word script?

Not always. Write dates, numbers, names, and the actual request the way you will say them. Explanations and examples can live as entry lines that you expand in your own words. On camera, a script that still sounds like a notice is easier to notice, so even the full sentences should be spoken aloud and tightened first.

In a meeting, should I look at the lens or at people’s tiles?

If you are addressing the whole group, looking at the lens is closer to eye contact for everyone watching. If you are in a one-to-one exchange, looking at that person on the screen often feels more natural, even though everyone else will see your gaze shift. Do not force one rule on both situations. In either case, keep the script close enough that it does not pull your eyes down to the desk.

If the webcam and microphone tests work, can I start recording?

Not on that basis alone. The web tools only show that this browser is receiving a picture or an input signal. They do not verify another app’s permissions, cropping, or mirroring, or what the other person actually sees and hears. After you stop the test, do a short check in the app you will use.

Can I record in sections and edit them together?

Yes, if you are making a recorded message or a video you will edit later. Start each section on a complete entry line, so a cut does not land in the middle of a sentence. For a live meeting, a livestream, or anything you cannot trim later, rehearse the piece as one pass.
