How to prepare and rehearse a five-minute work update

Make the next decision clear before you write the opening. Build a short status update, check its timing, and rehearse aloud without rushing through the important parts.

Published 2026-11-23T18:06:07+08:00

A five-minute work update does not need to cover everything you did. It needs to explain where the work stands, what could change the plan, and what you need from the people listening.

Write the decision or takeaway first. Choose progress that supports it, explain the main risk, and make the next action explicit. A word budget can expose an overloaded draft. A synthetic preview can help you notice awkward wording. Neither replaces delivering the update aloud with a timer.

Decide what the meeting needs from you

Before writing an introduction, finish four notes:

  • Bottom line: Where does the project stand against the plan?
  • Progress: Which one or two changes support that judgment?
  • Risk: What could affect the next stage, and what would trigger a change?
  • Ask: Who needs to decide or provide what, and by when?

“We need a decision on the pilot scope so we can schedule the next test” gives the room more direction than “Here is what we worked on this week.”

Keep background only when it changes how the audience will understand the decision. Move the rest to supporting material rather than reading a task log aloud.

Check whether the five-minute slot includes questions. If it does, plan less than five minutes of prepared remarks. Do not write a full five-minute script and assume questions come afterward.

Give each part a time budget

This is one suggested allocation, not a tested standard. It budgets four minutes and thirty seconds for prepared delivery and thirty seconds for additional slide changes, reactions, or planned interaction.

PartSuggested budgetWhat it should accomplish
Bottom line and purpose30 secondsState the status and the issue for today
Relevant progress90 secondsGive one or two pieces of evidence
Main risk60 secondsExplain the impact, trigger, and response
Decision or support needed60 secondsName the action, owner, and deadline
Next step and close30 secondsRepeat the agreed action and what follows
Extra allowance30 secondsAbsorb additional transitions or interaction

The total is five minutes. The allowance can be used throughout the update, not only at the end. It is already inside the five-minute limit.

Do not invent a risk to fill the outline. If nothing needs escalation, say so and explain the next assumption you will check. An easy decision does not need a minute of padding either.

Rewrite for a listener, not a project document

A listener cannot reread the first half of a sentence while you finish the second half. Split sentences that hide the action behind qualifications.

Before:

Following the completion of the initial requirements alignment and subsequent cross-functional consultation, the pilot proposal has reached a preliminary state of convergence, subject to the availability of the necessary resources for validation.

After:

We have agreed on a pilot covering two workflows. Before we can test it, we need a business owner to confirm the session time. Today, we need to name that owner and ask for an answer by Wednesday.

The two workflows and Wednesday deadline are invented details for this example. In your own draft, use only the scope, owners, and dates you can verify. Do not add false precision just to make a sentence sound stronger.

Look for phrases that delay the point:

  • Replace “undertake further alignment” with the action, such as “ask the business owner to confirm.”
  • Replace “there is some uncertainty” with a condition, such as “if the sample does not arrive by Wednesday, next week's test will move.”
  • Expand an unfamiliar abbreviation on first use. Write important numbers the way you intend to say them.

Keep natural transitions if they help people follow the argument. You are preparing a spoken explanation, not removing every conversational word.

Estimate a budget, then adjust it to your delivery

Open the speech length estimator and choose Time to word budget. For this example, manually set:

  • Target duration: five minutes.
  • English pace: 140 words per minute.
  • Extra allowance: thirty seconds.

That gives (300 − 30) × 140 ÷ 60 = 630 words.

Treat 630 as a budget under those assumptions, not a target you must fill. Explaining a chart, emphasizing a result, or waiting for a response can leave room for fewer words. The chosen rate is an adjustable example, not a standard speaking speed.

The estimator's published example table currently uses 130 English words per minute, which produces 585 words with the same allowance. To reproduce this article's calculation, enter 140 yourself. This article is not claiming that 140 is the tool's current default.

Chinese uses a different unit. At 200 Chinese characters per minute with the same thirty-second allowance, the budget is 900 characters. Do not treat 900 Chinese characters as 900 English words.

For mixed Chinese and English, use Script to time and calculate each part at its own pace. Four hundred Chinese characters at 200 per minute take two minutes. One hundred and forty English words at 140 per minute take one minute. Together with a thirty-second allowance, the estimate is three minutes and thirty seconds.

Your pace should include ordinary breathing and short pauses. Use the extra allowance only for time that is not already included, such as a planned slide change or audience interaction. Do not count the same pause once in your measured pace and again in the allowance.

The estimator asks you to write out numbers and symbols as you plan to say them. Follow its input guidance rather than deleting meaningful numbers to obtain a shorter result.

Try a short fictional update

The following is a fictional teaching example, not a customer story or a report of real results. It is deliberately short so you can try the process. It is not a complete five-minute script, and it has not been timed by a human speaker.

Today, we need to confirm the pilot scope. I recommend keeping the two agreed workflows and not adding another test.

This week, we reviewed the first round of feedback and revised the instructions. The next dependency is confirming when the business team can join the test.

The main risk is the sample schedule. If we do not have the sample by Wednesday, we will need to reschedule next week's test. We can review the existing material first, but that will not replace testing with the sample.

I need two answers today: whether we are keeping the current scope, and who will confirm the sample by Wednesday. Once those are clear, I will update the test plan and tell the people affected.

Use it to practice selecting a takeaway and making an explicit ask. Then replace it with your own material. Do not paste confidential work into an unverified environment.

Listen for wording, then rehearse in your own voice

You can paste the sample into the speech script preview, choose the main language and a browser-reported local voice, and press Play. Listen for long sentences, abrupt transitions, and numbers or names that need to be written differently.

Synthetic playback duration is not your speaking duration. A relative rate of 1.0 does not mean 140 words per minute. The tool uses one voice for the whole script, so mixed-language passages can be misread. If no matching local voice is available, skip the preview and read the draft aloud yourself. A browser's local-voice flag is not an independently audited guarantee of fully offline processing.

Scripts do not transfer between tools. Keep your own copy and paste it manually. The preview does not use your microphone, so it cannot check your recording setup or assess your pronunciation.

Now use an ordinary clock or timer for three different rehearsal passes:

  1. Deliver the whole update. Speak at a normal volume and use the materials you will actually show. Start the timer when you begin. Keep going through minor mistakes and note the final elapsed time. This real run already includes pauses and slide changes. Do not add the allowance again to the measured result.
  2. Cut before speeding up. Remove repeated context, an oversized example, or details that do not change the decision. Protect the conclusion, the main risk, and the ask. Do not solve an overloaded script by rushing through every sentence.
  3. Practice recovering. Look up and pause at a sensible point, then find your next sentence. Check that you can finish the ask without restarting the entire update. If it still runs long, revise and time it again.

Write down which section expanded, not just that the total was “over five minutes.” For your final run, use a similar device position, speaking distance, and set of materials. Unexpected questions or a changed room can still alter the timing.

If you use a teleprompter, know how to pick up manually

Oralo is available for iPhone and iPad. Before relying on it in a meeting, follow the voice-following setup guide with a short passage. Match the speech language, complete the readiness check, and practice pausing and choosing a sentence to continue from.

Voice following is not a promise that your place will never be lost. Background noise, other speakers, distance, language, and system models affect recognition. Returning automatically to any distant paragraph is not guaranteed. Know the manual sentence controls, and keep manual reading as an option when speech recognition is unavailable.

A successful rehearsal is not simply a piece of software reaching the last line. It is delivering the update within the agreed time while making the judgment, risk, and request clear enough for a listener to repeat.
