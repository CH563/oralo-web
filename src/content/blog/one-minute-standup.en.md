# How to give a one-minute standup update without rambling

A daily standup is usually about 15 minutes for the whole team. Once several people have spoken, you may have about a minute. If you narrate every meeting, document, and unfinished task from yesterday, it is hard for anyone to hear what actually changed, what you will move today, and whether you need someone else.

This is not a shorter version of a formal status update. Before it is your turn, write a few sentences you can finish: the progress, the next step, and any blocker the team needs to know.

## First, decide whether this is a standup or a longer update

A standup helps colleagues adjust today’s plan before they go back to work. It usually has no slides, and it does not need a personal introduction or a recap of the project; the team already shares that context.

If you actually have about five minutes and need to present a judgment and a request, use [How to prepare and rehearse a five-minute work update](https://oralo.fun/en/blog/five-minute-work-update/) for structure and timing. If you will also show slides, separate what people should read from what you will say, as in [How to turn your slides into a script you can actually say](https://oralo.fun/en/blog/from-slides-to-script/). This guide is only about the spoken minute.

The 2020 Scrum Guide describes the Daily Scrum as a 15-minute event for the Developers. Its purpose is to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the upcoming planned work. Developers may choose any structure or technique, as long as the event stays focused on that progress and produces an actionable plan for the next day of work. Identifying impediments and reducing the need for other meetings are listed among its effects. The 2020 update notes also say this edition removed the prescribed questions from the Daily Scrum.

Many teams still prepare their own update around three questions. Atlassian’s standup guide describes the structure they often use: what I worked on yesterday, what I am working on today, and what is blocking me. It also says it helps to walk in knowing what you will say. That is their practice guidance, not a rule for every team, and it is not a requirement in the 2020 Scrum Guide.

This article treats “progress, next step, blocker” as a structure you can write down in advance. If your team already has a fixed prompt, or walks the board instead of speaking person by person, keep that format and simply write the lines you will say.

## Write three sentences, then decide whether anything else is needed

Before you speak, write one sentence for each of these:

1. **Progress.** Since the last standup, what did you finish, or how far did you get? Mention what changes someone else’s work today, not a list that only proves you were busy.
2. **Next step.** How far do you plan to get today, especially if the work will cross, hand off to, or compete with someone else.
3. **Blocker.** If you have one, say what is missing, whom you need, and what you hope will happen after the meeting. If you do not, say so clearly. You do not need to invent a difficulty.

Then check whether you have packed a design discussion, a long explanation, or a complaint into that minute. In the standup, name the problem and the person who can help. Leave the detailed conversation for afterward, so one issue does not take over the meeting.

Also check:

- Write numbers, interface names, permission names, and abbreviations the way you will say them. The first time you use an abbreviation others may not know, add the wording they already understand.
- Make a request specific: “I need the payments admin to grant refund access on the test account after this,” not “permissions still need a look.”
- Do not pack background, progress, and a request into a single sentence. If someone misses the second half, they may only hear that you are “still working on it.”

## A short example shows the difference

The following is a fictional teaching example. It does not describe a real team, project, or access process. It only contrasts a rambling update with three sentences you can finish in about a minute.

**Before:**

> Hey everyone, I will just give a quick recap of my day yesterday. It was pretty busy, I was in a few meetings in the morning, then I went back over that earlier requirement, and I also fixed some small issues, so overall things are moving along. Today I will keep going on the same area and I will follow up if anything comes up. Oh, I am also tidying some docs and may need to align with a few people, I will take another look.

**After:**

> Yesterday I finished the error messages on the payment confirmation page. Once design adds the empty-state illustration, it can go to testing.
>
> Today I will start the refund-path integration. This afternoon I will hit the reconciliation API and need to confirm the fields with the data team.
>
> I am blocked because the test account still cannot issue refunds. Please can the access team grant that after this meeting? I will prepare the sample requests in the meantime.

The revised version leaves people with a finished piece of work, a likely hand-off later today, and a clear request after the meeting. “Payment confirmation page” and “refund access” belong only to this example. When you edit your own lines, replace them with a status and a request you can stand behind.

If someone asks a follow-up while you are speaking, finish the sentence the standup still needs, then offer to continue after the meeting. For returning to your next line after a detour, see [How to find your next line after you go off script](https://oralo.fun/en/blog/get-back-on-script/).

## Time about a minute, then cut it in your own voice

Once the lines are written, paste them into the [speech length estimator](https://oralo.fun/en/tools/speech-length/) to see whether they are likely to run long. The estimate is not your actual speaking time.

A standup usually has no slides or demo, so do not add the 30-second slide allowance used in that tool’s examples. The figures “200 Chinese characters a minute” and “130 English words a minute” are adjustable examples, not a standard pace, and they do not guarantee you will finish on time. With those English settings and no extra allowance, a one-minute budget is 130 words. Most first drafts are longer than that, so cut until you can finish, then time a complete pass in your own voice.

If a sentence still sounds like a daily report, or you are unsure of a name or a number, preview the wording in [Hear your script before you speak](https://oralo.fun/en/tools/speech-preview/). How long the synthetic voice takes is not how long you will take. The tools do not carry a script from one page to another, so keep your own copy before you leave. Standup notes often include internal project names, interfaces, and account details. Do not paste confidential material into a website tool.

Then say the three sentences out loud, at your normal volume, and time them. If you run long, cut background and task lists first, rather than making the blocker vaguer. If your team is large and each person only has about half a minute, compress each item to a shorter sentence and keep the blocker specific.

Both the 2020 Scrum Guide and Atlassian’s guide describe the whole Daily Scrum or standup as a period of about 15 minutes, not a rule that every person must fill a minute. The “one minute” in this article is a starting point for your own preparation. Use the time your team actually has when it is your turn.

## A few practical questions

### If I have no blocker, do I still need a third sentence?

Yes. A brief “nothing is blocking me” is enough. Use the time for the progress and the next step, rather than inventing a problem that sounds more important.

### If I cannot finish in a minute, is my pace too slow?

Look first for task lists and background. Cutting sentences that do not change anyone else’s plan for the day usually helps more than speaking faster. After you time the lines in your own voice, compress them to the time your team actually has. Do not lock in a delivery date you cannot stand behind just to sound further along.

### Our team posts updates in chat instead of speaking. Do I still need a script?

Some teams work asynchronously. If you still speak, keep using “progress, next step, blocker” for the lines you will say. If you only write a message, the same structure still helps. This article does not review chat tools, and it does not treat a written update as a replacement for a spoken standup.
