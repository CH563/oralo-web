# How to turn your slides into a script you can actually say

For many work updates, the deck comes first. On the day, it is tempting to read each bullet as it appears. Your audience can usually finish the slide faster than you can say it, so they are already on the last point while you are still working through the second.

The job is not to copy the slide text into a script you then recite. It is to write a spoken draft that does what the slide cannot do on its own: explain the judgment, say what the numbers mean, and take the room to the next page.

## Give each slide one job before you write a line

Open the deck you already have. Before you copy anything, write one sentence for each slide: after this page, what should people remember or decide?

If you cannot write that sentence, the slide is probably doing two jobs, or it is backup material. Split it, or move anything you do not plan to say off the presented page, then write the script.

In a 2021 guide to slide design in *PLOS Computational Biology*, Kristen M. Naegle suggests that each slide carry one main idea, and that the heading state that idea, rather than a label such as “Progress” or “Data.” The same piece treats “about a minute a slide” as a planning check for overload, not a rule you must hit, and not a formula for how many slides you need.

Once each slide has a job, check whether the whole deck still serves the decision or update you came to make. Details that do not change the judgment can live in an appendix or a follow-up note.

If you are preparing a short status update, the structure and timing in [How to prepare and rehearse a five-minute work update](https://oralo.fun/en/blog/five-minute-work-update/) still apply. This article starts from a deck you already have, and focuses on turning it into something you can say.

## Keep the audience's text and your spoken line apart

The words on a slide are there for a glance. Your script should add what the slide cannot finish: why the point matters, what a figure is meant to show, and who needs to do what next.

A 2023 Toastmasters article on slide presentations describes a familiar room: when several points appear at once, people may already be reading the fifth while you are still speaking the second. Let the slide hold the judgment or a few guide words, and put the explanation in what you say.

The same PLOS guide notes that reading and listening to speech use related language channels, so an audience often has to choose one, or do both poorly. That is a design warning from the literature, not a test we ran. In practice, keep short guide words on the slide, and speak in full sentences. The slide can still show the takeaway at a glance. It should not also carry the process, the request, and the transition, ready to be read aloud.

If something is on the slide, plan how you will point to it. If you are not going to talk about a number, a footnote, or an extra chart, leave it off the presented page.

## Rewrite one fictional slide, then compare

The following project-update example is a teaching fiction. It does not describe a real team, result, or notice. It is only here to show the difference between reading the bullets and writing a speakable script.

**Slide title, before:** Progress update

**Bullets, before:**

- Completed stakeholder alignment
- Pilot scope preliminarily finalized
- Sample timing remains uncertain
- Next: proceed with validation and notify stakeholders

**Script, before:**

> This week we completed stakeholder alignment, the pilot scope has been preliminarily finalized, sample timing remains uncertain, and next we will proceed with validation and notify stakeholders.

This version restates the slide. Listeners can already read those lines, and they still do not know what you recommend or what you need from them today.

**Slide title, after:** Keep the pilot to two workflows until we confirm sample timing

**What stays on the slide:**

- Proposed scope: two workflows
- Decision needed: sample timing by Wednesday

**Script, after:**

> I want this slide to settle one thing: we should keep the pilot to two workflows, and not add more this week. What you see here is a recommendation, not a decision that has already been made.
>
> The plan is now narrow enough to test. The thing that can still change the schedule is sample timing. If we do not have samples by Wednesday, next week’s test will have to move.
>
> So I need two confirmations today: that we will hold the scope at two workflows, and who will reply on sample timing by Wednesday. After that, I will send the revised test plan.

The later version turns the judgment, the condition, and the ask into lines you can say on their own. The slide keeps only the two things the room should look at. “Two workflows” and “Wednesday” belong to this made-up example. When you adapt the method, use a scope and a date you can actually stand behind.

Use the same approach with a chart: say the conclusion the figure is there to support, then point to one place to look, such as the change in the right-hand column. Do not read every axis label unless that label is the point of the discussion.

## Write the sentence that gets you to the next slide

People stall on “Let’s go to the next slide” when the deck has no spoken entry into the following page. Before you rehearse, write an opening line for each slide, and a transition you can say before you change pages.

In the example above, after the scope recommendation, you might say:

> That’s the scope I am asking for. The next slide looks at how the timing risk would change the schedule.

The transition should aim at the job of the next slide, not summarize the previous one again. If you want to add an example that is not in the script, decide how you will return to the next entry line before you leave it. For returning after a detour, see [How to find your next line after you go off script](https://oralo.fun/en/blog/get-back-on-script/).

Check these points:

- The first sentence on each slide can stand on its own, without relying on a half-finished thought from the previous page.
- Dates, names, and figures are written the way you will say them, so you are not rewriting them as you change slides.
- Planned asides live in a margin note, such as “optional example here,” rather than a second script you have to memorize.
- Speaker notes are a good place to keep the job of the slide, the transition, and any figure you must say accurately. They often still read like a document. Speak them out loud and revise, and make sure the audience never sees the notes pane by mistake.

## Time the talk with the deck, then rehearse out loud

After you shorten the draft, paste it into the [speech length estimator](https://oralo.fun/en/tools/speech-length/) to see whether the explanations and transitions are likely to run long. The estimate is not your actual speaking time, and it is not a word count you have to fill. Time for slide changes, pointing at a chart, or waiting for the room should go into extra reserve. A reading-speed estimate alone will not tell you whether the talk fits.

If a line still sounds like a document, paste it into [speech script preview](https://oralo.fun/en/tools/speech-preview/) and listen for wording only. The time a synthetic voice takes is not the time you will take, and the preview speed is not a speaking score. The tools do not carry your draft between pages, so keep a copy before you leave. Do not paste confidential material.

Then rehearse with the actual slides, in three passes that do different jobs:

1. **First pass: deliver the talk with the real slide changes.** Speak at a normal volume. If you stumble, keep going and note the total time, including page turns and pauses to point at a figure.
2. **Second pass: cut, rather than rush.** Remove background, repeated explanations, and details that do not serve the slide’s job. Keep the judgment, the condition, and the ask. Do not rely on talking faster to fit the limit.
3. **Third pass: look up, then continue.** At a sensible point, look at the room or the camera, then pick up from the next entry line. Confirm that you can finish the ask without rereading the current slide.

It is more useful to note which slide ran long than to remember only that the whole talk was over time. Use the same setup and materials you expect on the day for the last pass.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a word-for-word script?**

Not always. Write dates, numbers, scope, and the actual request the way you will say them. Explanations and examples can live as entry lines that you expand in your own words. Do not treat the slide bullets as your only prompt, especially when those words were written for the audience to read.

**Are PowerPoint or Keynote speaker notes enough?**

Notes are a good place to keep the job of each slide, the transition, and any figure you must say accurately. They often still read like a document, and a display or mirroring mix-up can put the notes in front of the audience. Rehearse the spoken lines on their own, and confirm that the audience side shows only the slides.

**If a slide is mostly a chart, should the script include every number?**

No. Write the conclusion the chart is there to support, and plan one place to point. Leave data you will not discuss off the presented slide, and do not read the rest for completeness.
